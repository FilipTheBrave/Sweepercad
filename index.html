Free to use. Mac: macOS 12.3 or later; Linux: Ubuntu 22.04 or similar and newer. The first time you open it: on a Mac, right-click the app and choose Open (on macOS 15 and later: System Settings → Privacy & Security → Open Anyway); on Windows, unzip it, run SweeperCAD.exe and choose More info → Run anyway; on Linux, unpack it and run ./SweeperCAD (the README inside lists any system libraries it needs). Not sure which Mac you have? Apple menu → About This Mac: "Apple M…" is Apple Silicon, "Intel" is Intel.

Videos

0, the idea: most 3D software picks a tool - extrude, revolve, loft or sweep. SweeperCAD has one: a profile along a spine, with a scale graph and an end profile. Cube, cone, ball and torus morph one into the next by moving those handles. Everything is a sweep - hence the name.

Profile, spine, scale graph, booleans, fillets and arrays in 50 seconds - every picture made by SweeperCAD itself.

Part 2, the deeper tools: editing live in 3D, drawing, start and end profiles, twist and roll, chains that morph, placing on surfaces, blends, measuring, lattices and references.

Part 3, non-destructive hard surface: a mech arm built from 35 nodes - intersect, subtract, arrays, blends - then a part dragged and the very first profile changed, and everything after it follows.

Part 4, work with what you have: a mesh as a reference, the section plane cutting it, five cuts becoming a stack of linked sweeps that rebuild it as editable CAD - then the same mesh worked on directly as a polygon part, read as a smooth CAD surface, and a STEP imported and slotted.

Part 5, check it: look inside with the section slider, the numbers (size, surface, volume, weight in a material), walls thinner than you allow, a bracket held and pushed with the stress on it, and zebra stripes that show whether a blend is really smooth.

Part 6, precise - loose when you want it, exact when you need it: drag points and handles right in 3D, then place on a surface, measure and keep a distance, and link profiles across nodes; the sketch editor's live dimensions, angles, constraints and corners (sharp, chamfer, round, bezier); the dials and the pivot; and variable-radius fillets to G2.

Part 7, shape it: a profile built from several shapes - pick a preset, add it, and the outline is their live Boolean, every shape still editable; draw on surfaces and fill a closed loop with a texture that follows the curve, carve it with a stroke; drop parts and let them settle; a route that finds its own way around; patch holes in a scanned mesh and sweep it into smooth CAD; the casting check - where a part sets last and how it fills from the gate - with a runner routed from the gate; and machining: toolpaths for a 3-axis router, simulated on the stock, checked, then G-code.

Part 8, math, names & links: every number field is a small calculator - type 360/3 and it is 120; use another object's own numbers by its name (tube.length + 2, tube.radius + 1 for a clearance) and names of your own, and everything that uses them follows; click a part to drop its number into the field; and the measure tool now links rotation and scale between two parts - with their offset kept, turn or grow either one and the other follows.

Part 9, unfold: lay a part out flat at true size to print and cut. Paper and card come out exact, with glue tabs, numbered seams and fold lines; fabric and leather as panels with a stretch map, seam allowance and notches. Place your own seams and darts, keep any automatic split joined, then print at 100 % (the 50 mm bar checks it), or save a PDF or an SVG for a cutter or laser.

Part 10, a personal message: why SweeperCAD exists, what I promise, and how you can help.
